## What Is a Balance Sheet?

A balance sheet is a fundamental financial statement that provides a snapshot of what a company owns, what it owes, and the total value contributed by its owners at a specific point in time. While the Profit and Loss (P&L) statement tracks operational performance across a period, the balance sheet evaluates solvency and long-term financial health.

The fundamental accounting equation must always remain in equilibrium:
**Assets = Liabilities + Shareholder Equity**

> Example: When a manufacturing firm invests ₹150 Crore into modernizing its production facility, that acquisition appears under Non-Current Assets. It is financed either through external debt (a Liability) or through retained earnings and capital reserves (Shareholder Equity).

## The Three Core Components

Every corporate balance sheet consists of three primary sections:

:::compare
Component | Definition | Common Balance Sheet Items
Assets | Economic resources owned that generate future cash flows | Cash reserves, inventory, property, plant, equipment
Liabilities | Financial debts and operational obligations owed to third parties | Bank loans, trade payables, statutory tax liabilities
Equity | Residual net interest owned by equity shareholders | Paid-up share capital, retained earnings, reserves
:::

## Key Solvency and Liquidity Ratios

:::facts
Current Ratio: Benchmark is 1.5x to 2.0x for adequate working capital
Debt-to-Equity: Prefer values below 1.0x to limit insolvency exposure
Cash-to-Debt Ratio: Verifies short-term debt repayment security
Return on Capital Employed: Look for consistent returns exceeding 15%
:::

## Step-by-Step Balance Sheet Evaluation

:::steps
Review the Debt-to-Equity trend over the past 3 to 5 financial years.
Compare Current Assets against Current Liabilities to confirm near-term liquidity.
Examine Reserves and Surplus to verify historical retention of net profits.
Inspect Trade Receivables against revenue growth to confirm genuine cash collection.
Identify Contingent Liabilities in the annual report notes for unrecorded litigation risks.
:::

## Pros and Cons of Balance Sheet Analysis

:::proscons
+ Highlights solvency hazards, leverage exposure, and structural weaknesses
+ Identifies companies with robust balance-sheet moats capable of surviving downturns
- Represents historical static data rather than forward-looking operating momentum
- Off-balance-sheet arrangements and aggressive capitalisation can mask true risks
:::

:::

## Common Analytical Mistakes

- Evaluating profitability exclusively while ignoring debt maturity schedules.
- Applying identical financial ratios to capital-intensive sectors (utilities) and asset-light models (software).
- Disregarding the footnotes in financial statements, which detail contingent guarantees and leasing liabilities.

## Conclusion

Analyzing a balance sheet protects investor capital against corporate distress. Prioritize enterprises with manageable leverage, expanding reserve surpluses, and conservative working-capital cycles.
